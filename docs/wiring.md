## Wiring Instructions
THE ROVER IS COMPLETELY SOLDERLESS. IT IS DESIGNED TO NOT REQUIRE A PCB, BUT RATHER EASILY SOURCED PARTS ONLINE. There is nothing but a bit of cutting wires/sliding them into screw terminals or WAGO lever nuts. ##### A GENERAL WARNING AND/OR A WORD TO NOTE BEFORE WE BEGIN. UNLESS YOU ARE TESTING YOUR CIRCUIT, DISCONNECT IT FROM THE BATTERY. IT DOES NOT MATTER IF THE POWER SWITCH IS OFF, OR EVEN THE BUCK CONVERTER'S SWITCH, IF THE BATTERY IS CONNECTED POWER CAN BE STORED IN THE BUCK CONVERTER'S CAPACITOR AND A) POWER WILL DRAIN FASTER THAN YOU THINK AND B) YOU MIGHT HAVE SPARKS WHILE YOU ATTACH YOUR COMPONENTS. WHENEVER YOU ATTACH SOMETHING, MAKE SURE THE POWER IS OFF. WHENEVER TURNING OFF POWER, START DOWNSTREAM UP (THE POWER SWITCH, THEN THE BUCK CONVERTER, THAN THE SPADE TERMINAL CONNECTORS THEMSELVES -- MAKING SURE TO TOUCH THE _PLASTIC_ NOT THE METAL TO RISK INJURY).

##### WHEN WORKING WITH STRIPPING THESE WIRES, I HIGHLY RECOMMEND USING THE STRIPPING TOOL TO MAKE AN INCISION THREE QUARTERS OF A FINGERNAIL FROM THE EDGE, AND THEN USING A SEPARATE BLADE TO FULLY REMOVE THE INSULATING PLASTIC LAYER (SINCE THE WIRE IS FINELY STRANDED, ANY MAJOR INCISION/TUGGING WITH THE WIRE STRIPPER WOULD SEVERELY DAMAGE THE WIRE BY REMOVING ITS STRANDS). I ALSO HIGHLY RECOMMEND SEATING EACH WIRE INTO THE YELLLOW ATTACHMENT AS MUCH AS POSSIBLE BEFORE CRIMPING ON THE BOTTOM PART (AND ON THE CENTER PART): IE, AS IN THE EXAMPLE BELOW, WHERE YOU CAN SEE __TWO__ MARKS FROM THE CRIMPING TOOL. I ALSO HIGHLY RECOMMEND TWIRLING THE WIRE A BIT (NOT TWISTING, SINCE THAT CAN ALSO DAMAGE THE WIRE, BUT "COMPACTING THEM" THROUGH SMALLER ROTATIONAL MOVEMENT) BEFORE SEATING INTO THE WAGO LEVER NUTS (WHERE THEY MUST BE PUSHED IN AS FAR IN AS POSSIBLE).

This can be done by taking each male-JST connector, and directing that to a female-female wire, and leading that to another male-JST connector (keeping the ground and signal wires the same, but exchanging the hot/positive lead for a voltage line out of another WAGO lever nut. Okay, so getting started with wiring. Remember to charge your battery while you set up everything (I recommend cutting up a few wires and beginning to strip them while you wait for the battery to charge a bit, although it ships near 50% so you can also get started immediately on this starter circuit).
Note that I call this a "starter circuit." It has the exact same wiring layout as the official wiring for the rover, but you haven't gotten to that yet so this is simply an incremental test to ensure all the parts work. You are to use no more than 1M of the wire (because the remaining meter will be used for the actual rover).

This is the "end result" before power injection, which is a WHOLE issue in of itself. We'll work our way up to it.
Firstly, two wires need to be crimped with a female spade terminal so everything fits nicely. You can see the spade terminals as the small yellow flat things. One of these will connect to the battery's positive lead, one to the ground or negative one. The 20A ANL fuse will connect DIRECTLY to the battery's positive lead via one of the two wires above, while the GND wire will go down the battery into one of the buck converters (you have three, pick one and wire the GND to it's IN- screw terminal). Meanwhile, the other end of the ANL fuse will lead to the IN+ screw terminal. It is at this point, you may take your multimeter and place its red lead in the Voltage port (usually, multimeters have three ports, I recommend the one labeled something along the lines of "mA") and the black lead in the COM port

<img width="830" height="466" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/3b123187-b571-4507-9336-ed944a320cb9" />

You may then place the red/positive lead into the OUT+ screw terminal and the black/negative lead into the OUT- screw terminal. Adjust CV (with a screwdriver) until you get a number roughly around 12V. This will serve as the power for your servo. Then, disconnect it when you are done.

Now, after that, your OUT+ lead of the buck converter will connect directly to one end of your Daiertek power switch (stripped, of course). The OUT- lead of the buck converter will connect directly to a GND wire. To understand why this specific layout was chosen, you need to understand: you provide power with the hot lead (the positive side) which carries current through an electric potential (a difference in voltage). Your goal, for all your electronics, is to make sure the positive lead wires somehow through a part, and then current flows from that positive lead to a negative or a ground port on that same part (where the voltage drop between the positive and negative ports allows for your current flow) and powers your device. Your power switch, by connecting to the buck converter and a WAGO lever nut, creates a "power rail," where any wire connected to that and a part which is similarly connected the "ground rail" (which your ground wire, which is connected to the OUT- of the buck converter and its own WAGO lever nut) and power can be provided. There are four red wires that connect to the power rail (ie, distribute power): the first is connected to another buck converter's IN+ lead (one that is to be used to provide power to the Raspberry Pi), and the other three are connected to their own WAGO lever nut (where, from each WAGO lever nut, power will be supplied to the appropriate servo, power injection, which can be seen through the image below). There are four black wires that connect to the ground rail, for the same reason: one acts as ground for the buck converter (and is inserter to the IN- lead), and three others connect to WAGO lever nuts to distribute ground to each servo. A common ground between the servo, after all, is necessary: the signals the bus servo adapter provides depend heavily on the voltage difference between the hot and ground leads, so a common voltage source MUST be accompanied by a common ground. You may fine tune the buck converter here so that you set 5V (or a slightly higher value, maybe 5.05 or 5.1V) as the CV on the second buck converter. The remaining slots are for wires that will directly go to the serial bus servos (not modeled here, but included in the image above, since I highly recommend NOT doing this step until you are absolutely positive everything is perfect).

You may wonder why this "power injection" is even necessary. In a daisy-chained configuration, all current required downstream is pulled upstream. When there is only one source, all this current goes through the VERY THIN JST PH wires (which are scarcely rated for more than 3A) and severe voltage browning occurs (ie, power is lost and suddenly, none of your servos are moving when you need them too). You need to make sure each servo gets its own independent power, and this reduces current through wire and ensures everything is fine. According to Waveshare, using the bus servo adapter in this way (to only run signals) is perfectly acceptable and extremely common (and thus, requires no major change in the code). The goal is, then, to make sure GND and the HOT leads are similarly _shared_ (ie, they are from the same source and maintain the same relative voltage difference, so that signals are accurate, which is why a single major power/gnd rail is created -- a "common" source allows for GND to be shared, and similarly for the HOT lead) and _individual_ (ie, no more than 2A every goes through ANY of the wires -- and if so, only for small amounts of time and VERY short distances).

Once you are done with your test circuit, I highly recommend waiting for prints and finishing [assembly](/docs/assembly.md), or at the VERY LEAST, finishing the [electronics set-up](/docs/electronics-setup.md) before beginning your official build so all of the wires can be cut to size and so you can properly place all of your electronics wherever you have space (or, for that matter, need to place them) on the build. Part of the reason for the MANY MANY MANY hex cut-outs (instead of simply reducing weight, something possible through a basic infill pattern such as 3d-honeycomb, cubic, or gyroid) is so electronics can be gently secured via screws and the multiple through holes.
